# Business Field Pack

Follow `[[phdude-core]]`. This pack refines vocabulary and review questions for business and
management research; it does not change the core evidence rules.

## What counts as evidence here

- **Firm-level data** - financial statements, market data, operational metrics.
- **Survey data** - employee, customer, or manager responses to structured instruments.
- **Case studies** - documented, sourced accounts of a firm's decisions and outcomes.
- **Archival/secondary data** - industry reports, government statistics, prior studies.

A single case study or anecdote from one firm is `candidate` evidence for a general claim
about "firms" or "managers," not `supported`.

## Common methodologies

- **Cross-sectional survey** - a snapshot of firms/managers/customers at one point in time.
- **Panel/longitudinal study** - the same firms or respondents tracked over time.
- **Case study** - in-depth analysis of one or a small number of firms.
- **Archival/econometric analysis** - secondary financial or market data with statistical
  controls.

## Review questions

- Is the sample of firms or respondents representative of the population the claim is about,
  or a convenience sample from one industry/region?
- Are financial metrics normalized for firm size, industry, and time period before comparison?
- Does the design support the causal language used, or only correlation among firms
  (e.g., is there a natural experiment, instrument, or matched control group)?
- Are confounds like firm size, industry, and macroeconomic conditions controlled for?
- Is the claim scoped to the studied market/region, or generalized beyond it without support?
- For survey-based claims, is response rate and non-response bias addressed?

## Epistemic norms

Business research is often cross-sectional or case-based, so most findings license
"associated with" or "co-occurs with" language, not causal claims, unless the design includes a
natural experiment, instrumental variable, difference-in-differences, or randomized field
experiment. A single case study supports a claim about that firm or a hypothesis for further
testing, not a generalization to "firms" or "the industry." When a claim mixes practitioner
framing (e.g., "best practice") with empirical findings, keep the two separate: state what the
data show, then label any prescriptive recommendation as interpretation, not as a demonstrated
result.
