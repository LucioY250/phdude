# Humanities Field Pack

Follow `[[phdude-core]]`. This pack refines vocabulary and review questions for humanities and
interpretive research; it does not change the core evidence rules.

## What counts as evidence here

- **Primary sources** - texts, documents, artifacts, and their direct quotation.
- **Secondary scholarship** - prior interpretations, critical editions, historiography.
- **Close reading / textual analysis** - argument grounded in specific passages, with
  location (page, line, folio) cited.
- **Archival documentation** - provenance, dating, and authentication of a primary source.

An interpretive claim without a cited passage or document is `candidate`, not `supported` -
"supported" here means the argument is traceable to specific textual or archival evidence.

## Common methodologies

- **Close reading** - detailed textual analysis of a passage or work.
- **Historiographical method** - situating a claim against prior scholarship and sources.
- **Discourse/textual analysis** - patterns of language, framing, or narrative across texts.
- **Archival research** - locating, dating, and authenticating primary documents.

## Review questions

- Is the primary source properly contextualized - dated, attributed, and situated in its
  original setting - rather than read anachronistically?
- Are competing interpretations of the text or event acknowledged, not silently dismissed?
- Is the argument grounded in specific textual evidence with citations (page/line/folio),
  rather than paraphrase or summary presented as analysis?
- Does the analysis distinguish the source's claim (what the text says) from the researcher's
  claim (what the researcher argues the text means)?
- Where scholarly consensus exists, is the claim's relationship to that consensus stated
  (aligned, departing, filling a gap)?

## Epistemic norms

Interpretive claims in the humanities are argued from evidence density and internal coherence,
not statistically tested, so wording should signal interpretive confidence rather than
statistical confidence - "suggests," "can be read as," "supports the interpretation that,"
rather than "proves" or "demonstrates." Reserve unhedged, plain assertions for well-established
textual or historical facts (documented dates, attested authorship, recorded events); an
interpretation of meaning, motive, or significance should almost always carry a hedge. When an
interpretation departs from majority scholarly consensus, say so explicitly rather than
presenting it as settled.
